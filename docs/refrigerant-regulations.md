# HVAC Refrigerant Regulations in Florida (2026)

This guide covers the current refrigerant regulations affecting HVAC systems in Florida, including the R-22 phaseout, R-410A transition, and emerging low-GWP alternatives.

## R-22 (Freon) — Fully Phased Out

### Current Status
- **Production and import banned** since January 1, 2020 (EPA Clean Air Act, Title VI)
- **Existing stock only** — remaining R-22 is recycled/reclaimed, prices have risen 400%+ since 2015
- **Not illegal to use** — operating an existing R-22 system is still legal, but sourcing refrigerant for repairs is increasingly expensive

### What Homeowners Should Know

| Situation | Recommendation |
|-----------|---------------|
| R-22 system, works fine | Continue using. No legal requirement to replace |
| R-22 system, needs recharge | Get a quote for recharge vs replacement — recharge costs $75-150/lb (systems use 6-15 lbs) |
| R-22 system, major repair needed | Almost always more cost-effective to replace with a new R-410A or R-32 system |
| R-22 system, 15+ years old | Plan for replacement — parts availability is declining |

### Retrofit / Drop-In Replacements

Some contractors offer R-22 "drop-in" replacements (R-407C, R-422D, etc.). Be aware:

- May void manufacturer warranty on remaining components
- Requires oil change in some systems (mineral oil → POE)
- Performance may not match original design specs
- Often a temporary measure — full system replacement is the long-term fix

## R-410A — Current Standard (Being Phased Down)

### Current Status
- **Still available** and the most common residential refrigerant
- **AIM Act (2020)**: Mandates 85% reduction in HFC production by 2036
- **EPA Rule (2024)**: New residential/commercial AC systems manufactured after January 1, 2025 must use refrigerants with GWP ≤ 700 (R-410A GWP = 2,088)
- **Existing R-410A systems**: Can continue to be manufactured, serviced, and recharged — no ban on the refrigerant itself for existing equipment

### Timeline

| Year | What Happens |
|------|-------------|
| 2025 | New equipment must use low-GWP alternatives (≤700 GWP) |
| 2026 | R-410A still widely available for service/recharge |
| 2030+ | R-410A supply may tighten as production quotas decrease |
| 2036 | 85% reduction in HFC production from baseline |

### What This Means for Homeowners

- **Buying a new system in 2026?** You'll likely get R-454B or R-32 — these are the R-410A replacements
- **Have a working R-410A system?** Keep it. R-410A will be available for service for many years
- **R-410A system needing replacement?** New systems will use R-454B/R-32, which is fine — your contractor handles the transition

## R-454B (Opteon XL41) and R-32 — The Future

### R-454B
- **GWP**: 466 (78% lower than R-410A)
- **Mildly flammable** (A2L classification)
- **Backed by**: Carrier, Daikin, and most major US manufacturers
- **Florida-ready**: Works well in high-ambient-temperature environments

### R-32
- **GWP**: 675 (68% lower than R-410A)
- **Mildly flammable** (A2L classification)
- **Backed by**: Daikin, Mitsubishi, and widely used in Asia/Europe since 2013
- **Higher discharge temperature** — requires compressor design changes

### A2L Safety Requirements (Florida)

Both R-454B and R-32 are classified A2L (mildly flammable). Florida Building Code and UL 60335-2-40 require:

1. **Refrigerant leak detection** built into the indoor unit
2. **Maximum charge limits** based on room size (prevents dangerous concentrations)
3. **Self-contained safety shutoff** if leak detected
4. **Installer certification**: HVAC technicians must complete A2L-specific training

These safety measures are built into the equipment by the manufacturer — homeowners don't need to add anything separately.

## EPA Section 608 Certification

All technicians working with refrigerants in Florida must hold EPA Section 608 certification:

| Type | Covers |
|------|--------|
| Type I | Small appliances (under 5 lbs refrigerant) |
| Type II | High-pressure systems (residential AC, heat pumps) |
| Type III | Low-pressure systems (large commercial chillers) |
| Universal | All of the above |

**Homeowner tip**: Ask your technician to show their EPA 608 card. Any legitimate HVAC tech will have one. It's a federal requirement, not optional.

## Florida-Specific Requirements

### Proper Recovery
Florida law (and federal law) requires contractors to recover refrigerant before disposing of equipment. Venting refrigerant carries fines up to $44,539 per day per violation.

### Reclamation
Used refrigerant must be sent to an EPA-certified reclaimer. Contractors cannot resell recovered refrigerant between customers without reclamation.

### Record Keeping
Contractors must maintain records of refrigerant purchases, usage, and recovery for a minimum of 3 years.
