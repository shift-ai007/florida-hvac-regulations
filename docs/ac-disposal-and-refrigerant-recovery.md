# AC Disposal & Refrigerant Recovery Rules in Florida

When an old air conditioner is replaced, the part most homeowners never think about is what happens to the unit being hauled away. There are real federal and state rules governing how the refrigerant is recovered, how the equipment is disposed of, and who is legally allowed to do it. Getting this wrong isn't just an environmental problem — it can expose a homeowner to liability and is a common sign of an unlicensed or cut-rate operator.

This reference explains the rules in plain language so you know what a properly handled change-out looks like.

> **Disclaimer:** This guide is general information, not legal advice. Rules change and local ordinances vary. Verify current requirements with the EPA and your county before acting.

## Federal Rule: EPA Section 608

The core requirement comes from **Section 608 of the federal Clean Air Act**, enforced by the EPA. Its central rules for disposal:

- **Refrigerant must be recovered before disposal.** It is illegal to knowingly vent refrigerants (R-22, R-410A, and the newer A2L refrigerants like R-454B and R-32) into the atmosphere during service, maintenance, or disposal.
- **Recovery must be done by an EPA-certified technician** using certified recovery equipment. Section 608 certification is a federal requirement — the technician removing refrigerant from your old system must hold it.
- **"Final disposal" responsibility.** The rule places obligations on whoever takes the last step before the appliance is scrapped. For appliances that still contain refrigerant when picked up, the person recovering it must be certified and use approved equipment.

The practical takeaway: a legitimate contractor will recover the refrigerant from your old system *on-site* before the unit leaves your property, and will keep records of having done so.

## Who Is Allowed to Do the Work in Florida

Florida layers its own licensing on top of the federal rule. HVAC work — including removing and replacing a system — must be performed by or under a state-licensed contractor (a **CAC** Class A/B air-conditioning license or **CMC** mechanical license). Recovering refrigerant additionally requires the individual technician to hold EPA 608 certification.

So a compliant change-out involves two credentials working together:

| Credential | Who Issues It | What It Covers |
|-----------|---------------|----------------|
| Florida CAC/CMC license | FL DBPR | Legal authority to perform HVAC installation/removal |
| EPA Section 608 certification | EPA (via approved orgs) | Legal authority to recover refrigerant |

If a "handyman" offers to swap your system cheaply, ask which of these they hold. Usually the answer reveals the problem.

## Disposal of the Equipment Itself

Once refrigerant is recovered, the remaining equipment is largely **scrap metal** — copper, aluminum, and steel. In Florida:

- Recovered-and-evacuated units can typically go to a **scrap metal recycler**. Many recyclers require a tag or documentation certifying refrigerant was removed; some will refuse a unit that still has its service caps intact and pressure inside.
- **Capacitors and control boards** may contain materials handled as electronic or hazardous waste depending on the county. Reputable contractors separate these.
- **Permits.** A system change-out in South Florida counties (Miami-Dade, Broward, Palm Beach) requires a mechanical permit, and the permitted job creates a paper trail that the work — including proper removal — was done by a licensed contractor.

## What a Compliant Change-Out Looks Like

When you have a system replaced, these are the signs the disposal side was handled correctly:

1. The technician connects **recovery equipment** to the old unit and pulls the refrigerant before disconnecting lines.
2. The old unit leaves with **caps or tags** indicating it has been evacuated.
3. The job is **permitted**, and the contractor is licensed and insured.
4. You're given documentation — an invoice or work order — that reflects proper removal and recovery.

If a crew simply cuts the lines (releasing refrigerant with a hiss) and tosses the unit on a truck, you've witnessed an illegal venting — and likely hired someone cutting other corners too.

Beyond the environmental harm, improper disposal is a reliable indicator of an unlicensed operator. Hiring one can void manufacturer warranties on your new equipment, leave you without permit closeout (a problem when you sell the home), and expose you to liability if the work damages your property or violates code.
