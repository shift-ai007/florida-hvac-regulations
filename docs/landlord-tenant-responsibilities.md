# Landlord & Tenant HVAC Responsibilities in Florida

Florida's hot and humid climate makes air conditioning a health and safety issue, not just a comfort concern. Yet many rental agreements are vague about who pays for AC repairs, replacements, and maintenance. This guide covers Florida law, standard lease provisions, and practical steps for both landlords and tenants.

## Florida Law: No Statutory AC Requirement for Residential Rentals

Unlike some states, **Florida does not have a statute that explicitly requires landlords to provide air conditioning** in residential rental units. The Florida Residential Landlord and Tenant Act (Part II, Chapter 83, Florida Statutes) requires landlords to:

- Comply with building codes affecting health, safety, and housing
- Maintain roofs, windows, screens, floors, and plumbing in good repair
- Keep common areas clean and sanitary

Air conditioning falls into a legal gray area. **If a lease specifically promises AC**, the landlord must maintain it. If the lease is silent, the tenant may have limited recourse — but local building codes in South Florida (Miami-Dade, Broward, Palm Beach) effectively require habitable spaces to have functioning cooling.

## When the Lease Mentions AC

Most South Florida leases, especially for single-family homes and newer apartment complexes, explicitly include AC as an amenity. In these cases:

| Scenario | Responsibility | Legal Basis |
|----------|---------------|-------------|
| AC breaks down | Landlord must repair within **reasonable time** (7-14 days typical) | Breach of implied warranty of habitability (case law) |
| Routine maintenance (filter changes) | Tenant, unless lease says otherwise | Standard lease language |
| Annual professional maintenance | Landlord, if specified in lease | Lease terms |
| AC replacement due to age | Landlord's cost (capital improvement) | Not a repair — landlord's asset |
| Damage from tenant misuse | Tenant pays for repair | Florida Statute 83.51(1) |

**Important**: Florida courts have held that a lease promising "air conditioning" means the system must be maintained in working order. A landlord cannot simply unplug or disable a working AC and call it "not promised."

## The 7-Day Notice Rule

If the AC fails and the lease promises AC:

1. **Tenant gives written notice** to the landlord describing the problem
2. Landlord has **7 days** to begin making repairs (Florida Statute 83.56)
3. If not addressed, the tenant may:
   - **Withhold rent** (must deposit into court registry — cannot simply stop paying)
   - **Repair and deduct** (up to 1 month's rent, only if the problem is material)
   - **Terminate the lease** for material non-compliance

**For AC specifically**, many South Florida judges have interpreted the 7-day clock aggressively — a week without AC in July is considered a **constructive eviction** in some county courts.

## Who Owns the System

This matters more than most tenants realize:

- **Window units and portable ACs** bought by the tenant are the tenant's property
- **Central AC systems, mini-splits, and ductwork** are part of the real property — the landlord's asset
- **Detachable components** like smart thermostats installed by the tenant may be removable (check lease terms)

**For landlords**: A central AC system is a depreciable capital asset (27.5-year residential rental depreciation under MACRS). Replacing the system is a capital expense, not a repair expense, regardless of what the lease calls it.

## Common Disputes and How They're Resolved

### "It's cooling — just not below 78°F"

Florida's Building Code (residential) requires that an AC system be capable of maintaining **78°F indoor temperature at 80% design conditions**. If the system is sized and maintained properly but the tenant demands 72°F, the landlord has met the obligation. If the system cannot maintain 78°F due to a malfunction, the landlord must repair.

**Practical test**: Set thermostat to 72°F and let the system run for 2 hours on a 92°F day. If the indoor temperature doesn't drop below 82°F, the system is likely underperforming.

### Mold from AC Condensation

Florida Statute 83.51 requires landlords to maintain the premises in a condition that prevents mold. AC condensation issues (clogged drain lines, leaking air handlers, humidity) are the **landlord's responsibility** if caused by system design or maintenance failures.

**Tenant defense**: If the tenant can show they reported condensation or musty odors and the landlord did not address it within 7 days, mold damage claims may shift to the landlord.

### "The AC unit was destroyed by a hurricane — who pays?"

- **Windstorm damage**: Landlord's property insurance covers the AC unit (it's a building component). Tenant's renter's insurance covers lost food, hotel costs if the unit is uninhabitable.
- **Flood damage**: Neither policy typically covers AC equipment on ground level. Landlord may need flood insurance for ground-floor or outdoor units.
- **Deductible**: Landlord pays the property insurance deductible. Tenant's deductible on renters insurance covers their contents claim.

### Electrical Upgrades Required for New AC

When an old (pre-2010) AC fails and replacement requires upgraded electrical service (200A panel, new disconnect, R410A-compatible refrigerant lines), the **landlord bears the cost** — these are permanent improvements to the building. This is a frequent point of friction: a tenant expects the AC fixed in 2 days, but the electrical work takes 3-5 additional days with permits.

## Practical Guidance

### For Landlords

1. **Include AC maintenance in the lease**: Give tenants clear instructions on filter changes (monthly in South Florida) and quarterly condensate drain flushes. Some leases now include a $25 filter delivery service.
2. **Respond same-day to AC emergencies**: In June-September, a 7-day response risks constructive eviction claims. Most South Florida property managers target 24-48 hour AC response during cooling season.
3. **Budget for replacement**: Central AC systems in Florida last 8-12 years in coastal areas (salt corrosion, year-round operation). With replacement costs of $4,500-$9,000, landlords should plan capital reserves accordingly.
4. **Pre-season maintenance**: Schedule annual spring AC maintenance (April-May) before the cooling season peaks. This catches refrigerant leaks, capacitor issues, and coil problems before they become emergency calls.

### For Tenants

1. **Document everything**: Save photos of the thermostat showing rising indoor temperature. Keep written repair requests (text and email are better than phone calls).
2. **Change filters monthly**: In Florida humidity, a 30-day filter change is non-negotiable. A clogged filter can freeze the evaporator coil within hours.
3. **Know your rights**: If the lease promises AC, the landlord must provide it. If they refuse, contact the Florida DBPR or file in county court.
4. **Renters insurance covers loss of use**: If the AC fails and the unit becomes uninhabitable during summer, your renters insurance may cover hotel costs — but only if you actually relocate.

## HVAC Contractor's Role

Licensed HVAC contractors in Florida (CAC, RA, or CMC) are frequently caught between landlords and tenants during AC disputes:

- **Don't diagnose for dispute leverage**: Provide objective evaluations — temperature splits, superheat/subcooling readings, and static pressure measurements. Let the lawyers decide responsibility.
- **Document existing conditions**: Before starting work, photograph and note pre-existing damage, especially if the unit has been run without a filter or on frozen coils.
- **Know the permit rules**: AC replacement in Florida requires a permit. A tenant cannot pull an owner-builder permit on a rental property they don't own. Landlords must use licensed contractors for replacements.
