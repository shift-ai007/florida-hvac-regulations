# Florida HVAC Permit Inspection Checklist

When you pull a permit for an HVAC installation or replacement in Florida, the building department sends an inspector to verify the work before signing off. For many homeowners, this inspection visit is a source of anxiety — but knowing exactly what the inspector checks turns that uncertainty into confidence.

This guide covers what Florida building inspectors look for during HVAC permit inspections, how to prepare, and what constitutes a pass or fail. Requirements apply statewide under the Florida Building Code (FBC) with local amendments, so your specific municipality may have additional requirements beyond this baseline.

## When Is a Permit Inspection Required?

Florida law requires a permit and final inspection for:

- New AC system installation (new construction or replacement)
- Duct system modification or replacement (over 25% of total duct surface area)
- AC equipment relocation
- System capacity change (upgrade or downgrade in tonnage)
- Major electrical work associated with HVAC (new disconnect, subpanel, or wiring)

**Repairs and maintenance** — including compressor replacement, coil replacement, refrigerant leak repair, and component-level service — generally do not require a permit or inspection in most Florida jurisdictions as of 2026. However, some Miami-Dade municipalities require permits for compressor replacements, so always check with your local building department before authorizing work.

## Common Inspection Stages

Some Florida jurisdictions require multiple inspection visits:

| Stage | What's Inspected | When |
|-------|-----------------|------|
| Rough-in | Ductwork before drywall, refrigerant line set, electrical conduit | Before walls are closed |
| Final | Complete system installation, all code-required items | After system is operational |

Many Florida jurisdictions (especially smaller municipalities) combine both into a single final inspection. Miami-Dade and Broward County are more likely to require separate rough-in and final visits.

## What the Inspector Checks (Final Inspection)

### 1. Equipment and Installation (FBC Chapter 11, M1301–M1611)

The inspector verifies the installed equipment matches the permitted specifications:

- **Model and serial numbers match the permit** — If the installer substituted a different unit, the inspector will fail the inspection unless the change was approved via a permit amendment
- **AHRI certificate match** — The outdoor condenser and indoor evaporator/air handler must be a matched system listed on the AHRI directory with the SEER2 rating declared on the permit. An unmatched system (e.g., replacing only the outdoor unit without matching coils) fails inspection
- **Manufacturer installation instructions followed** — The inspector may spot-check clearances, line set sizing, and filter requirements against the installation manual
- **Outdoor unit clearance** — Minimum 12 inches from the condenser to walls/obstructions on the air intake side (most manufacturers require 18–24 inches); minimum 48 inches of clearance above the unit; 36 inches of service access on at least one side

### 2. Electrical Safety (FBC Chapter 34, NEC 440)

- **Disconnect within sight of the outdoor unit** — A lockable fused or non-fused disconnect must be installed within 6 feet of the condenser and visible from it. No exceptions
- **Disconnect label** — Permanently affixed label identifying the disconnect as the "air conditioner disconnect"
- **Proper wire gauge** — Feeder wire matches breaker size and unit nameplate MCA (Minimum Circuit Ampacity)
- **Branch circuit breaker** — Correctly sized per nameplate MCA and MOP (Maximum Overcurrent Protection). Standard residence circuits: 30A for 2-ton, 35–40A for 3-ton, 45–50A for 4-ton
- **GFCI protection** — 2023 FBC requires GFCI protection for outdoor outlets within 6 feet of the condenser (applies to units installed within 6 feet of a required outdoor receptacle)
- **Bonding and grounding** — Proper equipment ground conductor installed and bonded at both ends

### 3. Refrigerant System (FBC Chapter 11, M1403)

- **Line set insulation** — Suction line (large diameter) must be fully insulated with minimum 3/8-inch wall closed-cell elastomeric foam from the evaporator coil to the service valves. Insulation must be protected from UV damage if exposed outdoors
- **Line set support** — Refrigerant lines must be supported at minimum 6-foot intervals, not resting on the ground or in contact with masonry
- **Leak check** — The installer must provide evidence of a passing standing pressure test or electronic leak detector sweep. Some jurisdictions require a signed declaration
- **Service valves** — Both liquid and suction service valves must be accessible and not buried behind siding, enclosure panels, or landscaping

### 4. Condensate Drain (FBC Chapter 11, M1411)

- **Primary drain line** — Sloped minimum 1/4 inch per foot toward the termination point, with no sagging or low spots
- **Drain termination** — Must drain to an approved location (not directly onto a sidewalk, driveway, or street). Typical approved locations: landscaping bed, plumbing waste line with air gap, dry well. In Miami-Dade and Broward, condensate may NOT drain to the sanitary sewer system — it must go to a pervious surface
- **Drain line cleanout** — Accessible tee or plug fitting within 12 inches of the evaporator coil for cleaning
- **Safety float switch or auxiliary drain pan** — Attic installations require a secondary drain pan with a float switch that shuts off the system if the primary drain clogs. Some Broward municipalities now require float switches on ALL installations, including ground-floor units

### 5. Duct System (FBC Chapter 11, M1601)

- **Duct material** — Ductwork must be approved (UL 181 listed) and free of tears, kinks, or compression bends. Flexible ducts cannot exceed 5 feet in length per run in some jurisdictions
- **Support spacing** — Flex duct supported at minimum 4-foot intervals, rigid duct at 8-foot intervals
- **Connections** — Ducts must be mechanically fastened (not just taped) at all connections. The inspector will tug-test a few connections; if a section of duct comes loose, it's a fail
- **Leakage testing** — For larger systems (≥3 tons), many Florida jurisdictions require a duct leakage test showing ≤15% total leakage. This applies to both new construction and complete duct replacement

### 6. Combustion Air and Ventilation (FBC Chapter 7, M1307)

- **Fresh air intake** — New construction systems must have a code-compliant outdoor air intake or mechanical ventilation system. Replacement systems are generally exempt, but if the new system is ≥2 tons larger than the old one, makeup air may be required
- **Combustion air clearance** — Gas furnace installations must maintain proper clearance from combustibles per manufacturer specs and the National Fuel Gas Code

## What Happens During the Inspection

A typical HVAC final inspection takes 15–30 minutes. The inspector will:

1. Verify the permit is posted at the job site (required by law) and all required fees are paid
2. Check model/serial numbers against the permit
3. Open the disconnect and confirm proper labeling and wire sizing
4. Measure line set insulation thickness and check for gaps
5. Cycle the system through cooling and heating modes (if applicable)
6. Pull the filter and check it's the correct size and type
7. Measure the temperature drop (delta T) across the evaporator to confirm proper operation — target 16–22°F
8. Look for visible signs of installation quality: secure fasteners, clean electrical connections, properly sealed duct connections

## Common Inspection Failures (and How to Avoid Them)

| Failure | Frequency | Prevention |
|---------|-----------|------------|
| Dirty filter at inspection | Very common | Install a clean filter 24 hours before inspection; note the filter size on the unit label |
| Missing or loose disconnect label | Common | Order permanent labels with any permit; don't rely on handwritten tape |
| Line set insulation gaps at coil connection | Common | Use mastic or UL-listed tape to seal insulation joints at the evaporator and condenser connections |
| Condensate drain not primed (overflow switch trips) | Moderate | Pour water into the drain pan before the inspection to prime the trap and confirm drainage |
| AHRI certificate not on site | Moderate | Print the AHRI certificate at permit application time and tape it inside the electrical panel cover |
| Float switch not tested | Moderate | Manually lift the float to confirm it interrupts the thermostat signal before the inspector does |

## After the Inspection

**If you pass**, the inspector signs the permit card or issues an electronic approval. Your county will update the property's permit records within 2–6 weeks. Keep the approved permit and the AHRI certificate in your home records — they're valuable documentation when selling the property.

**If you fail**, the inspector issues a correction notice listing each deficiency. You typically have 30–90 days to correct, re-inspect, and close the permit. Failure to close a permit within the municipality's timeframe can result in liens against the property or difficulty transferring title at sale.

## Selecting a Contractor Who Passes Inspections

A contractor's inspection track record tells you a lot about their work quality. Before hiring:

- Ask how many HVAC permits they've closed in your municipality in the last 12 months
- Request references from recent permit inspections in your jurisdiction
- Verify their license is active via the [Florida DBPR website](https://www.myfloridalicense.com)
- Confirm they carry workers' compensation and general liability insurance (required for permit work)
