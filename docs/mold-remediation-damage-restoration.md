# Mold Remediation, Damage Restoration, and HVAC Licensing in Florida

A reference guide for homeowners and contractors navigating the licensing boundaries between HVAC, mold remediation, and damage restoration work in Florida.

## The Regulatory Overlap

When a South Florida property suffers water damage — from a hurricane, burst pipe, or catastrophic AC drain line failure — the line between HVAC work, mold remediation, and general restoration is surprisingly blurry. Florida regulates each type of work under different license authorities, and crossing the boundary without the right credential can result in fines, stop-work orders, and voided insurance claims.

Understanding who can legally do what after water damage is essential for both homeowners hiring contractors and professionals working in adjacent trades.

## The Three License Categories

### 1. HVAC Contractor (CAC or CMC Class A, B, or C)

**Governing body**: Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR) — Construction Industry Licensing Board

The HVAC license covers:
- Installation, repair, and replacement of heating, ventilation, and air conditioning systems
- Ductwork and air distribution systems
- Refrigerant handling and disposal
- Thermostat wiring and controls
- Condensate drainage from HVAC equipment

**Limitations**: An HVAC contractor cannot bill for structural restoration, general construction repairs, or remediation work that extends beyond the HVAC system itself. They can remove and replace drywall or insulation that was _directly damaged by an HVAC component failure_ (e.g., a burst condensate pan), but only as an incidental part of the HVAC repair.

### 2. Mold Remediation Contractor (MRSR or MRSA)

**Governing body**: DBPR — Florida Mold Licensing Program

Two levels of mold licensure:

- **Mold Remediation Specialist (MRSR)**: Full license — can design and supervise mold remediation projects. Requires 1,000 hours of field experience, 14 hours of approved training, and passing a state exam
- **Mold Remediation Associate (MRSA)**: Restricted — can perform remediation work only under the supervision of an MRSR. Requires 14 hours of training and 60 hours of field experience within 2 years

**Scope**: Both licenses cover the physical removal, cleaning, and sanitization of mold-contaminated materials. This includes containment setup, HEPA vacuuming, antimicrobial application, and clearance testing coordination.

**Limitations**: A mold remediation license alone does NOT authorize the licensee to:
- Repair or replace HVAC equipment
- Repair structural damage caused by the water intrusion
- Perform general construction
- Handle refrigerant

### 3. General/Residential Contractor

**Governing body**: DBPR — Construction Industry Licensing Board

Covers structural repairs, drywall replacement, insulation, framing, and finish work after damage. A general or residential contractor is the only one authorized to rebuild walls, ceilings, and floors after water damage — but they cannot touch the HVAC system or perform mold remediation beyond incidental surface cleaning during construction.

## Real-World Scenarios

### Scenario A: AC Drain Line Floods a Ceiling

A clogged condensate drain overflows the indoor unit, water soaks through the ceiling drywall, and mold appears within 72 hours.

**Who can do what:**

| Task | Licensed to Perform |
|------|-------------------|
| Clear the drain line and repair the AC | HVAC (CAC/CMC) |
| Cut out wet drywall | General contractor OR HVAC (if incidental to AC repair) |
| Install new drywall and paint | General contractor |
| Test for mold and write protocol | Mold assessor (separate license — not HVAC, not MRSR) |
| Remove mold from framing | Mold remediation (MRSR/MRSA) |
| Replace ductwork soaked by water | HVAC (CAC/CMC) |

**Common violation**: An HVAC contractor patches the drywall, then removes visible mold from the attic framing. The drywall patch may be within scope (incidental to the AC repair), but the mold removal exceeds their license — and if the homeowner files an insurance claim, the carrier may dispute coverage because unlicensed mold work was performed.

### Scenario B: Hurricane Floods Ground-Floor AC System

A storm surge floods the first floor, damaging the air handler and ductwork.

**Who can do what:**

| Task | Licensed to Perform |
|------|-------------------|
| Replace flooded air handler | HVAC (CAC/CMC) |
| Replace flooded ductwork | HVAC (CAC/CMC) |
| Remove flood-soaked drywall (4 ft up the wall) | General contractor |
| Apply antimicrobial to framing | General contractor (non-structural treatment) OR MRSR (if mold is present) |
| Replace insulation in walls | General contractor |
| Rebuild electrical subpanel that was under water | Licensed electrical contractor |
| Test refrigerant lines for flood contamination | HVAC (CAC/CMC) |

**Common confusion**: Many homeowners assume their AC contractor will handle "everything in the affected area." In practice, the HVAC contractor is limited to the equipment and ductwork. The demolition, drying, flood remediation, and rebuild must be coordinated separately. A single restoration company that holds multiple Florida licenses (or subcontracts all the licensed trades) is the most seamless option.

### Scenario C: Homeowner Discovers Black Mold in Supply Ducts

Visible mold is growing on the interior of flex duct in an unconditioned attic.

| Task | Licensed to Perform |
|------|-------------------|
| Test mold type and airborne spore count | Licensed mold assessor (independent from remediator) |
| Remove and replace moldy flex duct | HVAC (CAC/CMC) |
| Clean and sanitize remaining metal duct trunk | HVAC (CAC/CMC) — but only mechanical cleaning. Chemical biocides may require MRSR |
| Seal duct boots and register boxes | HVAC (CAC/CMC) |
| Post-remediation clearance test | Licensed mold assessor |

**Gap to watch**: Flex duct that is visibly moldy must be **removed and replaced**, not cleaned. No amount of cleaning restores the non-porous inner lining. An HVAC contractor is the correct choice for duct replacement. But if the mold has spread to drywall or insulation around the duct boots, a mold remediator (MRSR) and general contractor become necessary.

## Requirements by Insurance Type

### Windstorm or Hurricane Claims

Florida law (627.7011 and 627.701) requires insurers to cover "direct physical loss." After a hurricane:

- **Emergency HVAC repairs** (tarping outdoor units, sealing exposed ducts) are typically covered without pre-authorization
- **Replacement of flood-damaged HVAC components** requires a licensed contractor's estimate as part of the claim
- **Mold remediation** is only covered if the mold resulted from a covered peril (e.g., rain from a hurricane-damaged roof). Florida statute 627.706 requires a separate mold endorsement or $10,000 minimum coverage for mold-only claims

### CPL (Commercial Property) and Flood Claims

- Flood damage to HVAC equipment requires a **separate flood policy** — standard homeowners policies do not cover flood, even from hurricane-driven storm surge
- The National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) covers HVAC equipment only if it's elevated above the Base Flood Elevation or located above the first floor. Below-grade or ground-floor installations in V or A zones are excluded
- Private flood insurers (Lloyd's, Neptune, PURE) may have more favorable HVAC coverage — but always check the "mechanical equipment exclusion"

## Compliance and Liability

### For Homeowners

- **Always verify three credentials** before the first contractor walks through the door: (1) HVAC license for AC work, (2) MRSR if mold is being "remediated" (not just cleaned), and (3) general contractor license if walls are being rebuilt
- **One company that claims to do all three** should be asked which specific license applies to each phase. It is legal for a properly diversified company to hold multiple licenses. It is not legal for one unlicensed entity to cross boundaries
- **Florida requires a separate mold assessor** (not the remediator) for the final clearance test. If your contractor handles their own clearance testing, the results may not satisfy insurance or legal requirements in a dispute. See [Florida Administrative Code 61-47](https://www.myfloridalicense.com/DBPR/mold-related-services/)

### For HVAC Contractors

- Know your scope of work before a water-damage service call. Removing saturated drywall that is physically blocking access to the air handler = incidental (probably within scope). Tearing out a full ceiling to dry the joists = structural (exceeds HVAC scope)
- If you encounter mold during an AC repair, stop work on the mold area and recommend a separately licensed MRSR. Your HVAC license covers mechanical work, not biological remediation
- Document everything: photos before/after, scope in writing, and clear language about what is and isn't covered by your license. Insurance disputes can arise months after the repair
- For licensed contractor verification, see our [license types and scope guide](docs/license-types.md)

## Questions to Ask Your Contractor

When hiring after water damage in South Florida:

1. Do you hold a Florida HVAC license? (CAC number required for AC work)
2. Do you hold a Florida MRSR license? (Separate from HVAC)
3. Who will do the mold clearance testing? (Must be an independent assessor)
4. Who is your general contractor for structural repairs? (If the damage extends beyond incidental access cuts)
5. Will a single point of contact coordinate all phases? One restoration coordinator is fine — but the actual work at each phase must be performed under the correct license
