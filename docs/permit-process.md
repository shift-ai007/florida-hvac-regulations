# The HVAC Permit Process in Florida

A step-by-step guide to how HVAC permits work in Florida, from application through final inspection.

## Overview

The permit process ensures HVAC installations meet Florida Building Code requirements. Your licensed contractor handles the entire process, but understanding it helps you track progress and hold your contractor accountable.

## Step 1: Application

**Who does it**: Your licensed HVAC contractor (CAC or CMC)

**What's submitted**:
- Permit application form (online or in-person at building department)
- Manual J load calculation showing proper equipment sizing
- Equipment specifications (model numbers, efficiency ratings)
- Scope of work description
- Contractor's license and insurance information
- Site plan showing equipment locations (sometimes required for new installs)

**Timeline**: Same-day to 3 business days for residential AC replacements. New construction can take longer.

**Fees**: $75 - $500 depending on scope and county (see county-specific information below).

## Step 2: Permit Approval

The building department reviews the application to verify:
- Contractor holds a valid, active license
- Equipment meets minimum efficiency standards (15 SEER2 in South Florida)
- Load calculation supports the equipment sizing
- Proposed work complies with current Florida Building Code

**Approval time**: Most residential AC replacements are approved same-day or next business day. More complex projects may take 3-10 business days.

## Step 3: Work Begins

Once the permit is issued:
- Permit must be posted at the job site (visible from the street for new construction)
- Work must start within 180 days of permit issuance (or permit expires)
- Only the licensed contractor named on the permit may perform the work
- Any changes to scope require a permit revision

## Step 4: Rough-In Inspection (If Applicable)

Required for:
- New ductwork installations
- Major duct modifications
- New construction HVAC rough-in

**What the inspector checks**:
- Duct sizing matches Manual D calculations
- Duct material and insulation meet code
- Support and hanging methods are adequate
- Condensate drain routing and slope
- Refrigerant line routing and protection
- Electrical rough-in for equipment

**Not required for**: Simple equipment changeouts (same location, same ductwork)

## Step 5: Final Inspection

Required for **all** permitted HVAC work. This is the most important step.

**What the inspector checks**:

### Equipment
- Model numbers match the permit application
- Equipment is properly secured (condenser on pad, air handler properly mounted)
- Clearances meet manufacturer requirements
- Disconnect switch within sight of outdoor unit

### Ductwork
- All connections sealed with mastic or UL 181 tape
- Insulation meets R-value requirements
- No excessive kinks or restrictions in flex duct
- Return air adequate for system capacity

### Refrigerant
- System properly charged (may check subcooling/superheat)
- No visible leaks at line set connections
- Line set properly insulated

### Electrical
- Dedicated circuit with proper wire gauge
- Disconnect sized correctly
- All connections secure and properly terminated

### Drainage
- Primary condensate drain properly sloped and terminated
- Secondary drain or float switch installed
- P-trap where required

### Performance
- System operates and cycles properly
- Air temperature differential across coil within specification (typically 15-20 degrees F)
- All zones receiving adequate airflow

## Step 6: Permit Closure

After passing final inspection:
- Inspector signs off on the permit
- Status changes to "Final" or "Closed" in the county system
- Record becomes part of the property's building history

**Timeline**: Inspector usually arrives within 1-3 business days of scheduling. Results same-day.

## Common Inspection Failures

| Issue | Frequency | Fix |
|-------|-----------|-----|
| Missing float switch on air handler | Very common | Install secondary safety switch |
| Duct connections not sealed | Common | Apply mastic sealant to all joints |
| Condensate drain slope insufficient | Common | Regrade drain line (min 1/8" per foot) |
| Disconnect not within sight of unit | Occasional | Relocate disconnect or install additional one |
| Equipment oversized vs load calc | Occasional | Replace with properly sized equipment |
| Missing permits for electrical work | Occasional | Pull separate electrical permit |

## How to Check Your Permit Status

### Miami-Dade County
- Website: [Building Permit Search](https://www.miamidade.gov/permits/)
- Search by address or permit number

### Broward County
- Website: [Broward ePermits](https://www.broward.org/Planning/Pages/default.aspx)
- Search by address, permit number, or contractor name

### Palm Beach County
- Website: [PBC EZPermit](https://www.pbcgov.org/pzb/building-division/)
- Search by address or permit number

## What If Inspection Fails?

1. Inspector documents the deficiencies
2. Contractor must correct all issues
3. Contractor schedules a re-inspection
4. Re-inspection fee may apply ($25-$75)
5. Process repeats until all items pass

**Your role as homeowner**: Do not make final payment until the permit shows "Final" status. An open permit can cause problems when selling your home or filing an insurance claim.
