# HVAC Permits and Transfer of Ownership: What Every Florida Home Seller Should Know

When selling a home in Florida, the buyer's title company and home inspector will review permits for major improvements — including HVAC replacements and modifications. Unpermitted HVAC work is one of the most common seller disclosure issues in South Florida real estate transactions. This guide explains what's required, what risks exist, and how to resolve permit gaps.

## What Requires an HVAC Permit in Florida

Under Florida Building Code (FBC) Chapter 1, Section 105, a building permit is required for:

- **New HVAC system installation** (replacing the condenser + evaporator + air handler as a matched system)
- **Replacing an air handler or furnace only** (changes the mechanical system configuration)
- **Relocating any HVAC equipment** (moving condenser, air handler, or ductwork)
- **Substantial ductwork modification** (adding, removing, or rerunning more than 10 linear feet)
- **Electrical work associated with HVAC** (new disconnect, new circuit, or panel modification)

**What does NOT require a permit**:
- Like-for-like repair of an existing system (compressor replacement, coil replacement, control board)
- Duct cleaning and sealing (without structural modification)
- Thermostat replacement (same type)
- Routine maintenance

## Why This Matters for Home Sales

### Title Company Requirements

Most Florida title companies now require proof of permits for any HVAC equipment replacement within the past 5–10 years. If permits were not obtained:

- The buyer's lender may require escrow holdback for permit resolution
- Title insurance may exclude coverage for unpermitted improvements
- The seller may be required to post a bond for permit compliance post-closing

### Home Inspection Findings

A standard Florida home inspection will flag:

- Missing permit stickers on the condenser nameplate (some counties issue a permit sticker)
- Disconnect or electrical work that doesn't match the permit record
- SEER rating mismatch between the installed equipment and county records
- Contractor license number not matching the installing company

### Insurance Implications

After closing, if unpermitted HVAC work causes a fire, flood, or structural issue, the homeowner's insurance claim may be denied based on the "work performed without permit" exclusion common in Florida homeowner policies. This is a growing concern as insurers tighten underwriting after recent hurricane seasons.

## Checking Permit History

### Florida Counties Online Search

Most South Florida counties offer free online permit search:

| County | Website | Search By |
|--------|---------|-----------|
| Miami-Dade | epermitting.miamidade.gov | Property address or folio number |
| Broward | permits.broward.org | Property address |
| Palm Beach | pbcgov.com/epermit | Parcel ID or address |

**What to search for**: Use keywords "HVAC," "mechanical," "air conditioning," and "A/C." Look for permit status "Finaled" (completed and inspected). Permits still showing "Open" or "Issued" have not passed final inspection.

### How Far Back to Check

HVAC equipment has a typical 10–15 year lifespan. County digital permit records generally go back to the early 2000s. If the home was built before 2000 and the HVAC was replaced but no permit exists online, the county may have paper records — or the work was done without a permit.

## Resolving Unpermitted HVAC Work

### Option 1: Retroactive Permit (Most Common)

Florida law allows homeowners to obtain a permit retroactively for work already performed, provided it meets current code at the time of inspection. The process:

1. A [licensed Florida HVAC contractor](https://ac-repair.today/) inspects the installation
2. Any code violations are corrected (improper disconnects, missing safety switches, oversized equipment)
3. The contractor applies for a "as-built" or "retroactive" permit
4. County inspector verifies compliance
5. Permit is finalized retroactively

Cost typically ranges from $500–$1,500 depending on corrections needed.

### Option 2: HVAC Certification Letter

Some counties (particularly Broward and Palm Beach) accept a letter from a licensed mechanical contractor certifying that the existing installation meets FBC safety standards. This is faster and cheaper than a full retroactive permit but may not satisfy all title companies.

### Option 3: Title Insurance Waiver

If obtaining permits is impractical (the installing contractor is no longer in business, the home sold multiple times since the work), the title company may issue a waiver or endorse the policy to exclude the unpermitted HVAC. This shifts risk to the homeowner but allows the sale to close.

## Seller Disclosure Requirements

Florida law (Florida Statute 475.278) requires sellers to disclose known defects — and material non-disclosure of unpermitted work can lead to legal liability post-sale. Real estate contracts in Florida increasingly include specific HVAC permit disclosure addenda.

**Best practice for sellers**: Before listing, obtain a permit history report and proactively disclose any gaps. Offering a $1,000 credit for permit resolution removes the issue as a negotiation point.

## What Buyers Should Ask

If you are purchasing a home in South Florida:

- Request the county permit history for the property before the inspection period expires
- Ask the seller: "Was the HVAC system permitted when it was installed?" (Get it in writing)
- If the system is 8+ years old with no permit record, budget $500–1,500 for potential permit resolution
- Verify the contractor license on the condenser nameplate against the Florida DBPR license database
