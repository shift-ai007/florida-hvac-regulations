# Filing a Complaint Against an HVAC Contractor in Florida (DBPR)

Most HVAC jobs go fine. But when a contractor does shoddy work, abandons a project, takes a deposit and disappears, or works without a proper license, Florida homeowners have a real avenue for recourse: the **Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR)**, which licenses and disciplines air-conditioning contractors statewide. This guide explains who DBPR can help, what it can and can't do, and exactly how to file.

> This is general educational information, not legal advice. For a dispute involving significant money, consult an attorney.

## Who Regulates HVAC Contractors in Florida

Air-conditioning, heating, and refrigeration contractors are licensed by the **Construction Industry Licensing Board (CILB)**, which operates under DBPR. The two license categories you'll see most often are:

- **CAC (Certified Air Conditioning Contractor)** — Class A and Class B, valid statewide
- **RAC / RAR (Registered Air Conditioning Contractor)** — valid only in the local jurisdictions where the contractor has registered

DBPR's authority is over the **license** and the licensee's professional conduct. It can investigate, discipline, fine, and ultimately suspend or revoke a license. Understanding that scope is important, because it shapes what a complaint can actually achieve.

## What DBPR Can and Cannot Do

**DBPR can:**
- Investigate violations of Chapter 489, Florida Statutes (the contracting law)
- Discipline licensees for incompetence, misconduct, abandonment, or fraud
- Pursue **unlicensed activity** — operating without the required license is a serious offense in Florida
- Impose fines and license sanctions

**DBPR generally cannot:**
- Force a contractor to refund your money or redo the work directly
- Award you damages (that's what small claims or civil court is for)
- Resolve a pure price disagreement where the work was done competently

For monetary recovery, homeowners often pursue DBPR discipline **and** a separate civil claim. In some cases involving a licensed contractor, the **Florida Homeowners' Construction Recovery Fund** may reimburse certain losses after a civil judgment — a reason to keep your documentation thorough.

## Before You File: Build Your File

A strong complaint is a documented one. Gather:

1. The **written contract**, estimates, and any change orders
2. **Proof of payment** — canceled checks, card statements, receipts
3. **Photos and video** of the defective or incomplete work
4. **All communication** — texts, emails, voicemails
5. The contractor's **name, business name, and license number** (verify it first — see below)
6. **Permit records** from your local building department, if a permit was required and pulled (or wasn't)

Always confirm whether the person you hired was actually licensed. You can look up any contractor on DBPR's public license search before — and after — a problem arises. ## How to File the Complaint

1. **Go to the DBPR complaint portal.** Complaints can be filed online through DBPR's website, or by downloading the **Uniform Complaint Form** and mailing it.
2. **Identify the licensee.** Provide the license number if you have it; DBPR can usually locate the licensee from the business name otherwise.
3. **Describe the facts plainly.** Stick to what happened and when. Attach your documentation.
4. **Submit and save your confirmation.** You'll receive a complaint number — keep it.

After submission, DBPR reviews the complaint for **legal sufficiency** (does it allege a violation it has authority over?). If sufficient, it's assigned to an investigator. You may be contacted for additional information. Investigations take time — often months — and the licensee gets an opportunity to respond.

## Reporting Unlicensed Activity

Unlicensed contracting is treated more seriously than a quality dispute. If someone performed AC work without the required license, DBPR has a dedicated **Unlicensed Activity (ULA)** process and can pursue citations and even criminal referral. This is also where homeowners are most exposed: an unlicensed "contractor" usually pulled no permit, carries no proper insurance, and may leave you liable for code violations.
