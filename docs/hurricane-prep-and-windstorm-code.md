# Hurricane Preparation and Florida Windstorm Code for HVAC

Florida's wind events are unlike anything else in the continental United States. A 130 mph gust will turn a poorly secured 200-pound condenser into a projectile. The Florida Building Code (FBC) treats HVAC equipment as a structural component for this reason — and the rules differ from what most mainland contractors are used to.

This guide explains what the windstorm provisions require for residential HVAC, what homeowners should verify before hurricane season, and what to do in the 72 hours bracketing a named storm.

## How the Wind Code Applies to HVAC

Outdoor equipment — condensers, heat pump outdoor sections, mini-split outdoor units, package units, and equipment platforms — must be anchored to resist the design wind pressure for the building's location. The relevant references in the 8th Edition (2023) FBC are Chapter 16 (Structural Design), Chapter 23 (Wood), and the Florida Mechanical Code Chapter 3 (Equipment Anchoring).

### Wind Zones in South Florida

Florida is divided into wind zones based on Ultimate Design Wind Speed (Vult):

| Region | Vult (mph) | Risk Category II |
|--------|-----------|------------------|
| Miami-Dade (HVHZ) | 175 | High Velocity Hurricane Zone |
| Broward (HVHZ) | 170 | High Velocity Hurricane Zone |
| Palm Beach (coastal) | 165–170 | Non-HVHZ |
| Palm Beach (inland) | 150–160 | Non-HVHZ |

**HVHZ** (High Velocity Hurricane Zone) means Miami-Dade and Broward operate under stricter rules than the rest of the state. Any HVAC installation in those counties must use HVHZ-approved methods, and inspectors will not pass a job that uses ordinary anchoring hardware.

## Condenser Anchoring Requirements

A modern split-system condenser weighs roughly 150 to 240 pounds. Wind uplift on an exposed unit can exceed 600 pounds of force during a Category 3 storm. The FBC requires anchoring that resists both lateral movement and uplift.

### Approved Anchoring Methods

1. **Concrete pad with hurricane straps**: The condenser sits on a 4-inch concrete pad, secured to the pad with galvanized straps that wrap up over the unit and bolt into pre-set anchors. The pad itself must be at least 4 inches thick and bear on undisturbed soil or a compacted base.

2. **Engineered roof platform**: Roof-mounted units (more common on commercial buildings) require an engineered platform that ties into the roof structure through the underlayment. The platform must carry a Florida Product Approval (FL#) number.

3. **Wall-mounted brackets**: Mini-split outdoor units on wall brackets must use brackets with an FL# rated for the building's wind speed. Generic brackets sold online are almost never approved for HVHZ counties.

### Non-Compliant Setups You'll See

Drive any older neighborhood in South Florida and you can spot violations from the street: condensers sitting on patio pavers with no straps, units bolted into rotten 2x4 platforms, brackets attached to stucco with masonry screws no deeper than half an inch. These setups will likely fail in a real wind event and will void homeowner's insurance windstorm coverage on the equipment.

## Florida Product Approval Numbers

Every piece of HVAC equipment installed in Florida is supposed to have a current FL# Product Approval. This is the state's way of tracking which models have been tested for wind, water, and impact in Florida-specific conditions.

You can verify an FL# at the Florida Building Commission's online database. If a contractor installs equipment without an FL#, that's a building code violation regardless of how the unit performs in normal weather. For more on permit-related compliance issues, see [permits.md](permits.md) and [building-codes.md](building-codes.md).

## Pre-Season Preparation (May Through July)

Before hurricane season ramps up, run through this list once. Most South Florida homeowners can do steps 1 through 4 themselves; step 5 is a job for a licensed contractor.

1. **Visually inspect anchoring**: Are the hurricane straps still tight? Do the bolts look corroded? Has the concrete pad cracked or settled?
2. **Clear the condenser perimeter**: Trim vegetation back at least 24 inches on all sides. Loose branches turn into projectiles.
3. **Check the disconnect**: The outdoor electrical disconnect should latch firmly. A wind-blown disconnect cover can short the contactor.
4. **Photograph the install**: Take date-stamped photos of the unit, anchoring, and surroundings. These photos are invaluable for insurance claims after a storm.
5. **Get a windstorm inspection**: A licensed HVAC contractor can verify anchoring meets current code and provide a written report. Some insurers offer premium credits for this documentation. South Florida homeowners can [schedule a pre-season inspection](https://ac-repair.today/services/ac-maintenance/) to check anchoring, refrigerant lines, and electrical connections before peak season.

## Pre-Storm Shutdown Procedure

When a named storm enters the cone with a 72-hour forecast for your area, follow this shutdown sequence:

### 72 to 48 Hours Before Landfall

- Pre-cool the home to 68 to 70°F. After the storm passes, restoring this temperature will take many hours.
- Top off your refrigerator and freezer. Pre-cooling reduces the load when power returns intermittently.
- Test the generator (if you have one) and confirm fuel supply. Most residential generators cannot run a full central AC; plan for the AC to be off during power outages.

### 24 to 12 Hours Before Landfall

- Turn off the AC at the thermostat and at the outdoor disconnect. This protects the compressor from voltage spikes when the grid fails or restores.
- Cover the outdoor condenser if you have a manufacturer-approved cover. **Do not** wrap the unit in plastic sheeting or tarps — these trap water and create projectile hazards.
- Bring loose patio furniture, pots, and lightweight objects inside or into a garage. Anything that can become airborne should be secured.

### After the Storm Passes

- Wait until conditions are safe to go outside (no standing water, no downed lines).
- Visually inspect the outdoor unit before turning anything back on. Look for impact damage, displacement on the pad, water inside the electrical compartment, or refrigerant line damage.
- If you see any damage, **do not** turn the unit on. A flooded compressor or damaged refrigerant line will fail catastrophically if energized.
- If the unit appears intact, restore power at the disconnect first, wait 5 to 10 minutes for the compressor crankcase heater to warm any liquid refrigerant, then turn the thermostat to cooling.

## Common Post-Storm Problems

In the two to three weeks after a major South Florida storm, HVAC failures cluster around a few specific issues:

- **Flooded outdoor units**: Salt water is the worst. Even after the water recedes, corrosion attacks the contactor, capacitor, and fan motor. Equipment that was submerged should be inspected before being energized.
- **Voltage damage from grid restoration**: As power returns to neighborhoods in phases, unstable voltage can fry control boards and compressors. A whole-house surge protector helps; turning the AC off at the disconnect is better.
- **Debris in the condenser coil**: Wind-blown leaves, sand, and roof granules pack into the outdoor coil fins. Performance drops noticeably until the coil is professionally cleaned.
- **Refrigerant line damage**: Falling branches or shifted equipment can crack the line set or fittings. Symptoms appear hours or days later as the system loses charge.

If your AC is not working after a storm, [emergency AC repair from a licensed South Florida contractor](https://ac-repair.today/services/emergency-ac-repair/) is often booked solid in the first week — schedule as soon as you know there's a problem.

## Insurance Implications

Florida windstorm policies usually have separate deductibles for named storms, typically 2% to 10% of dwelling coverage. HVAC equipment is covered under most policies, but the claim process has specific requirements:

- **Documentation**: Pre-storm photos and a current windstorm inspection report dramatically speed up claims.
- **Mitigation**: Insurers expect homeowners to take reasonable steps to prevent damage. Leaving the unit running during a Category 3 will be a coverage argument.
- **Replacement vs. repair**: Older units (15+ years) are often "totaled" because replacement parts are unavailable or repair costs exceed depreciated value.
