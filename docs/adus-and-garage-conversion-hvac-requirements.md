# HVAC Requirements for ADUs and Garage Conversions in Florida

Florida's 2023 passage of SB 102 (effective July 1, 2023) requires all local governments to allow accessory dwelling units (ADUs) in residential zoning districts. Combined with rising home prices and multigenerational living trends, this has triggered a boom in garage-to-living-space conversions and backyard cottage construction across South Florida.

Converting an unconditioned garage or building a new detached ADU presents specific HVAC challenges that differ from standard residential installations. This guide covers the Florida Building Code requirements, sizing considerations, and common pitfalls.

## What Qualifies as an ADU Under Florida Law

Under SB 102 and Florida Statute 163.31771:

- **Size limit**: Maximum 800 square feet (or 50% of primary dwelling, whichever is less)
- **Minimum size**: At least 150 square feet
- **Permitted**: Attached or detached structures on single-family lots
- **Prohibited**: Short-term rentals (less than 30 days) in ADUs — must be recorded as a deed restriction
- **Setbacks**: Subject to the same setbacks as the primary structure per local zoning

**Key consideration for HVAC**: Because ADUs are classified as dwelling units, they must comply with ALL Florida Building Code, Mechanical Code, and Energy Code requirements for habitable spaces — including Manual J load calculations, ventilation, and minimum efficiency standards. They are NOT "accessory structures" under the building code.

## Code Requirements for ADU HVAC

### 1. Manual J Load Calculation

Florida Building Code (FBC) requires a Manual J residential load calculation for any new or replacement HVAC system in a dwelling unit — and this includes ADUs. Common sizing mistakes:

| ADU Type | Typical Size | Typical Load | Recommended System |
|----------|-------------|-------------|-------------------|
| Garage conversion (insulated) | 400–500 sq ft | 0.75–1.25 tons | 12,000 BTU mini-split or small ducted system |
| Garage conversion (poor insulation) | 400–500 sq ft | 1.0–1.5 tons | 12,000–18,000 BTU mini-split |
| Detached ADU (new construction) | 600–800 sq ft | 1.0–1.5 tons | 18,000 BTU mini-split or 1.5-ton ducted |
| Attached ADU (addition) | 500–700 sq ft | 0.75–1.5 tons | Depends on shared wall/roof exposure |

**Most ADUs are oversized.** A 500 sq ft garage conversion in Miami-Dade with modern insulation, double-pane windows, and a shaded roof deck needs less than 1 ton of cooling — yet many contractors install 18,000–24,000 BTU units. An oversized mini-split short-cycles, fails to dehumidify, and runs inefficiently. Insist on a Manual J calculation before purchasing equipment.

### 2. Minimum SEER2 Requirements

2023 FBC requires:

- **Split systems**: Minimum 15.0 SEER2 (climate zones 1 and 2 — all of South Florida)
- **Single-package systems**: Minimum 15.0 SEER2
- **Ductless mini-splits**: Minimum 15.0 SEER2

Most mini-splits sold today exceed this (18–28 SEER2 is common), but budget units may not. Verify the SEER2 rating, not just the "SEER" rating — the Department of Energy transitioned to SEER2 in 2023, which uses a different test procedure.

### 3. Ventilation Requirements

FBC Mechanical Code (Chapter 4) requires mechanical ventilation for all dwelling units. ADUs are no exception:

- **Minimum ventilation rate**: 7.5 CFM per occupant + 0.03 CFM per square foot (ASHRAE 62.2-2022), or 0.35 air changes per hour, whichever is greater
- **For a 500 sq ft ADU with 2 occupants**: approximately 30–35 CFM continuous
- **Methods**: Exhaust fan, HRV/ERV, or a ducted supply with make-up air
- **Garage conversions**: If the ADU shares a wall with the garage (which was converted into living space), the remaining garage-to-ADU wall must be fire-rated per FBC requirements, and any ventilation intake must not draw from the garage space

**Garage conversion gotcha**: Many homeowners assume existing garage ventilation is sufficient. It is not — garages are not conditioned spaces and usually have no mechanical ventilation. You must add dedicated ventilation for the converted living space.

### 4. Condensate Disposal

FBC Mechanical Code Section 314 requires condensate from all cooling equipment to be disposed of in an approved manner — not simply drained onto the ground or into a garage floor drain.

For ADUs:
- **Gravity drain preferred** — if the unit is on an exterior wall or at ground level
- **Condensate pump required** — if the unit is on an interior wall, below grade, or in a detached structure without gravity access
- **Condensate cannot drain into sewage grinder pumps or sewage ejector pits** without an air gap
- **Garage floor drains** cannot be used for condensate disposal per FBC — they are typically connected to storm sewers or dry wells, not sanitary sewers

### 5. Electrical Requirements

FBC Electrical Code and NEC Article 440 apply:

- **Dedicated circuit** for the HVAC system (20A minimum for most mini-splits)
- **Disconnect required** within sight of the equipment
- **GFCI protection** for any outdoor unit (2023 NEC requires GFCI for outdoor HVAC equipment)
- **Garage conversion note**: If the existing garage subpanel lacks capacity, you may need to upgrade the service or add a new subpanel. A standard 12,000 BTU mini-split draws 6–10 amps, but the startup current can spike higher. Verify your panel has room for a new breaker.

## HVAC Options for ADUs

### Ductless Mini-Split (Most Common)

**Pros**: No ductwork required, zone control, high efficiency (18–28 SEER2), DIY-friendly options available (Mr. Cool, Pioneer), low profile
**Cons**: Visible indoor head unit, condensate pump maintenance, communication wire between indoor/outdoor required

**Cost**: $1,500–3,000 installed for a single-head system
**Best for**: Garage conversions, detached ADUs, small additions

### Small Ducted System (Central Air)

**Pros**: Invisible (ducts in ceiling or raised floor), central filtration, no indoor wall units, better air distribution
**Cons**: Ductwork design and installation cost, needs ceiling or floor cavity space, higher total cost
**Cost**: $3,500–6,000 installed
**Best for**: Attached ADUs where the primary home's system can be extended, or new construction ADUs with planned ductwork

### High-Velocity Mini-Duct System

**Pros**: Small 2-inch ducts fit in tight spaces, good humidity control, less invasive installation
**Cons**: Higher equipment cost, specialized installation expertise needed, louder than conventional systems
**Cost**: $4,000–7,000 installed
**Best for**: ADUs with zero attic space, historic structures, or where conventional ducts won't fit

### PTAC / Through-Wall Unit

**Pros**: Lowest upfront cost ($500–1,500 including installation), no outdoor condenser needed, self-contained
**Cons**: Lowest efficiency (10–13 EER), noisy, poor humidity control, short lifespan (5–8 years), visible wall unit, does not meet Florida building code minimum efficiency for a dwelling unit in most cases (check local amendments)
**Cost**: $500–2,500 installed
**Not recommended for**: Permanent ADUs that require full code compliance

## Special Considerations for Garage Conversions

Converting an unconditioned garage into living space creates specific HVAC challenges beyond what a new-build ADU faces:

### Insulation Requirements

Existing garage walls, ceilings, and floors must be brought up to current FBC Energy Code insulation levels:

| Surface | Minimum R-Value (Climate Zone 1) |
|---------|---------------------------------|
| Walls | R-13 (2x4) or R-15 (2x6) |
| Ceiling/roof | R-30 |
| Floor (over crawlspace) | R-11 |
| Garage door replacement wall | R-13 (if converting garage door to wall) |

If the garage slab is the floor, it must be insulated at the perimeter (R-5 vertical for 24 inches) and covered with finished flooring. A bare concrete slab acts as a thermal sink that makes HVAC sizing nearly impossible — the AC will run continuously in summer trying to cool a slab that's been at 90°F for years.

### Make-Up Air for Gas Appliances

If the ADU contains a gas water heater, gas dryer, or gas cooktop, and the original garage was vented through the garage door, the conversion removes that natural ventilation path. You must provide:

- **Direct combustion air** for sealed-combustion appliances (preferred)
- **Ducted combustion air** from outside for atmospherically vented appliances
- **Carbon monoxide detectors** — required by FBC for any dwelling unit with combustion appliances or an attached garage, but added ADUs with gas appliances must have CO detectors interconnected with the primary residence

### Fire Separation

FBC requires:
- **Fire-rated assembly** between the ADU and the primary dwelling with a 1-hour fire-resistance rating
- **Fire dampers** in any ductwork penetrating the fire-rated separation
- **Smoke alarms** interconnected with the primary residence
- **Duct detectors** in duct systems serving multiple dwelling units (rare in single-family ADUs but applies to duplex conversions)

## Permitting Process

All HVAC work in ADUs requires permits in South Florida:

1. **Building permit** for the ADU or garage conversion (includes HVAC scope)
2. **Mechanical permit** for the HVAC system (usually included in the building permit)
3. **Electrical permit** for the HVAC circuit
4. **Inspections**: Rough-in inspection for line set and wiring, final mechanical inspection

**AVOID**: Installing the HVAC system under a "handyman" permit or with no permit at all. ADU work requires permits because it creates a new dwelling unit. Unpermitted ADU HVAC work can:
- Void your homeowner's insurance
- Cause inspection failures when you sell the property
- Result in a lien or citation from the building department

## Utility Rebates for ADU HVAC

Some Florida utilities offer rebates for high-efficiency HVAC installations that can apply to ADUs:

- **FPL**: Rebates for qualifying high-efficiency AC systems (check current program — rebate levels change quarterly)
- **TECO**: Similar efficiency rebate programs
- **Duke Energy Florida**: Rebate programs for qualifying equipment
- **Federal**: 25C tax credit still applies — 30% of equipment cost up to $2,000 for Energy Star Most Efficient split systems and heat pumps installed in dwelling units (ADUs qualify as dwelling units)

## Common Mistakes

1. **Sizing from square footage alone** — "500 sq ft = 12,000 BTU" ignores orientation, window area, insulation, and ceiling height. A 500 sq ft garage conversion with a western-exposure glass door and zero ceiling insulation needs 18,000 BTU; the same space with modern insulation and northern exposure needs 9,000 BTU.

2. **Skipping the Manual J** — contractors who "know the size" without doing the calculation are guessing. Florida law requires the load calculation.

3. **Using a window unit or portable AC** — these do not meet FBC minimum efficiency or ventilation requirements for a dwelling unit. They also do not provide adequate humidity control for South Florida.

4. **Forgetting ventilation** — air-sealing a garage conversion without adding mechanical ventilation creates indoor air quality problems. South Florida's tight construction requires ASHRAE 62.2 compliance.

5. **Mini-split placement blocking windows** — in a small ADU, the mini-split head unit must be placed where its airflow doesn't blow directly on beds or seating areas. Plan placement before running the line set.

6. **No condensate pump backup** — for a detached ADU with a condensate pump failure during summer, the indoor unit will leak water and shut down on the safety float switch. A battery-backed condensate pump or a gravity-drain fallback prevents this.
