# Duct Leakage Testing Requirements in Florida

Duct leakage is one of the most overlooked causes of high energy bills and uneven cooling in Florida homes. The Florida Building Code (FBC) and Florida Energy Conservation Code have specific duct leakage testing requirements — but many homeowners and even some contractors don't fully understand them.

This guide covers what's required, who must test, how testing works, and what the results mean.

## Why Duct Leakage Matters in Florida

South Florida homes present unique duct leakage challenges:

- **Attic-installed ducts** (common in single-family homes) operate in 140°F+ attic conditions — leaks lose more energy in extreme temperature differentials
- **High humidity** means leaking ducts pull hot, moist air into attics and crawl spaces, accelerating mold growth and rust
- **Year-round AC operation** (8-10 months in South Florida) means leak losses compound faster than in seasonal climates
- **Leaky return ducts** depressurize conditioned spaces, pulling outdoor air, humidity, and pollutants into the home

A typical South Florida home with unsealed ducts loses **15-30% of conditioned air** through leaks. At current FPL rates, that's $300-$600/year in wasted energy.

## When Duct Leakage Testing Is Required

Florida's energy code has evolved significantly. Here's the current requirement:

### New Construction

All new residential construction in Florida **must** have duct leakage tested by a certified third-party rater. This has been required since the 2014 FBC Energy Conservation Code.

| Requirement | 2020-2023 FBC | 2023+ FBC |
|-------------|---------------|-----------|
| Total duct leakage | ≤ 8 CFM25 per 100 ft² conditioned floor area | ≤ 8 CFM25 per 100 ft² conditioned floor area |
| Leakage to outside | ≤ 5 CFM25 per 100 ft² conditioned floor area | ≤ 4 CFM25 per 100 ft² conditioned floor area |
| Test method | HERS or equivalent | HERS or equivalent |
| Penalty for failure | Must seal and retest | Must seal and retest until passing |

**CFM25** = cubic feet per minute measured at 25 Pascals of duct pressure, the standard test pressure.

### Existing Home Renovations

Duct leakage testing was historically not required for replacements and retrofits. However, the 2023 FBC update changed this:

- **Duct replacement**: If more than 40 linear feet of duct is replaced, the entire duct system must be tested for leakage
- **System replacement**: When replacing an air handler or condensing unit, duct leakage testing is **recommended but not mandatory** under state code — however, several local jurisdictions (Miami-Dade, Broward) now require it
- **Attic insulation upgrades**: If attic insulation is increased as part of an energy upgrade, duct leakage testing is required by the 2023 FBC to confirm ducts are sealed before insulating over them

### Duct Sealing Only

If ducts are being sealed (without replacement), post-sealing testing is required to verify the work:

| Leakage Before Sealing | Required Post-Sealing Target |
|------------------------|------------------------------|
| > 16 CFM25/100ft² | ≤ 12 CFM25/100ft² |
| 12-16 CFM25/100ft² | ≤ 10 CFM25/100ft² |
| < 12 CFM25/100ft² | ≤ 8 CFM25/100ft² |

## The Testing Process

### Equipment

Duct leakage testing uses a **duct pressurization system** called a Duct Blaster (or equivalent):

1. A calibrated fan connects to the air handler's supply plenum
2. All supply and return registers are sealed with temporary covers (foam blocks or tape)
3. The fan pressurizes the duct system to 25 Pascals
4. The fan's airflow meter measures the CFM needed to maintain 25 Pa — this is the total leakage

### Total vs Outdoor Leakage

Two measurements are taken:

- **Total leakage**: All leaks (supply, return, and leakage to unconditioned space)
- **Leakage to outdoors**: Only the leaks that escape to attics, garages, or crawl spaces (not leaks into conditioned rooms)

The outdoor measurement is the stricter standard because leaked air that escapes the conditioned envelope represents pure waste. Leaked air that stays within conditioned space (e.g., from a supply duct in a dropped ceiling within conditioned area) is less harmful.

### Typical Results

| Condition | Total Leakage CFM25/100ft² | Estimated Efficiency Loss |
|-----------|---------------------------|--------------------------|
| New construction (well-sealed) | 4-7 CFM25 | 5-8% |
| Average existing home | 10-16 CFM25 | 12-20% |
| Leaky (inadequate install) | 18-25 CFM25 | 20-35% |
| Very leaky (return leaks) | 25+ CFM25 | 30-50% |

South Florida homes with **mastic-sealed rather than tape-sealed joints** typically test in the 4-8 range. Homes where only foil tape was used (especially on flex duct) often test at 10-15.

## Common Failure Points

Duct leaks are not random. Experienced energy raters find the same failure patterns in South Florida:

### 1. Return Plenum Connections (50% of failures)

The return drop connects to the side or bottom of the air handler. If the gap between the drop and the cabinet is not mastic-sealed, air leaks at the connection. In Florida attic installations, this leak pulls 130°F+ attic air directly into the return — bypassing the filter entirely.

### 2. Flex Duct Connections to Metal Collars (30% of failures)

Flex duct attached to sheet metal collars with a zip tie and minimal mastic is the most common leak point. The correct method: slide the flex duct 2 inches onto the collar, apply mastic inside the flex duct liner, secure with a metal worm-drive clamp (not a zip tie), and seal the outer jacket with mastic.

### 3. Supply Boot Connections at Ceiling Drywall (15% of failures)

The metal boot that transitions from duct to ceiling register often has a gap between the boot flange and the drywall. In a conditioned space this is cosmetic. In a vented attic, it's a major leak.

### 4. Air Handler Cabinet Itself (5% of failures)

Lower-cost air handlers have cabinet panels that don't seal tightly. Some models leak 50+ CFM25 from the cabinet alone before any duct is connected.

## The Aerosol Duct Sealing Alternative

Traditional duct sealing involves manually accessing each joint and applying mastic — labor-intensive for existing ducts hidden behind walls or in finished attics. **Aerosol-based sealing (Aeroseal)** offers an alternative:

- A computer-controlled system pressurizes the duct with non-toxic polymer particles
- Particles accumulate at leak edges, sealing them from the inside
- Reduces leakage by 70-90% on average
- Cost: $1,500-$3,000 for a typical Florida home
- Works best on smaller, well-accessible duct systems
- Less effective on very large (>3-ton) systems with major structural leaks

## RECA Testing for Commercial Systems

Commercial HVAC systems in Florida follow different standards under ASHRAE and the Florida Mechanical Code. For commercial kitchens, healthcare facilities, and large retail:

- **RECA testing**: Refrigeration and Commercial Air Conditioning ductwork must be tested at 400% of design pressure (not just 25 Pa)
- **SMACNA standards**: Commercial duct seal classes require specific leak thresholds based on static pressure class
- **Commissioning documentation**: Required for LEED and Energy Code compliance on commercial new construction

## Practical Recommendations

### For Homeowners

1. **Ask for test results on new construction**: Before closing on a new-build home, request the duct leakage test report from the builder. This is your right — the test was done and paid for. If the builder can't produce one, ask why.
2. **Test after any major renovation**: If you've had ducts replaced or HVAC equipment swapped, consider a $200 diagnostic test. The energy savings from sealing identified leaks pay back in 1-2 cooling seasons.
3. **Look for signs of duct leaks**: Rooms that are significantly harder to cool than others, dusty supply registers, high humidity in specific rooms, and visible duct damage in the attic all suggest leaks.
4. **Seal before insulating**: If adding attic insulation, seal ducts first. Insulating over unsealed ducts hides the leaks and makes future sealing much harder.

### For HVAC Contractors

1. **Test before and after sealing**: Document the improvement. Pass rates and test numbers are a competitive differentiator for informed customers.
2. **Use mastic, not tape**: In Florida attic temperatures (140-160°F), standard foil tape adhesive fails within 1-3 years. Mastic or UL-181 tape is required.
3. **Don't rely on quick visual checks**: Leaks at return plenums and duct-boot transitions are invisible without testing. Your best installer's work can still fail a duct test.
4. **Account for flex duct aging**: Flex duct liner permeability increases with age. A 2014 installation that passed at 5 CFM25 may now leak at 10+ CFM25 due to liner degradation and tape failure.

## License & Certification Requirements

Duct leakage testing in Florida must be performed by:

- **RESNET HERS Rater**: Most common for residential new construction
- **BPI Building Analyst**: Common for existing home energy audits
- **Licensed HVAC contractor**: Can perform testing in-house but should have separate quality assurance
- **Commissioning agent**: Required for some commercial projects

Florida does not currently require a specific duct testing license, but using a HERS rater ensures consistency with code compliance documentation for permitting.
