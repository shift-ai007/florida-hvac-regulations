# Insurance Claims for HVAC Systems in Florida

When your AC system suffers damage — whether from a hurricane, lightning strike, fire, flood, or simple mechanical failure — understanding the insurance claim process can mean the difference between a smooth replacement and months of frustration. Florida's unique insurance environment adds layers of complexity that homeowners elsewhere don't face.

This guide covers how homeowners and commercial property insurance handles HVAC claims, what Florida law requires of insurers, and how to navigate the claims process effectively.

## Types of HVAC Insurance Claims

### Wind & Hurricane Damage (Most Common Florida Claim)

Florida's windstorm exposure creates a distinct category of HVAC claims:

**Covered damage typically includes:**
- Outdoor condenser unit damaged by debris impact during a named storm
- Compressor failure due to floodwater intrusion (requires flood insurance — see below)
- Condenser coil damage from wind-driven salt spray
- Damage from falling tree limbs or flying debris during a storm event
- Roof-mounted equipment damaged or displaced by wind

**Not covered without specific endorsements:**
- Gradual corrosion from salt air (maintenance issue, not sudden damage)
- Wear and tear from normal operation
- Pre-existing damage identified during a post-storm inspection

**Important:** Florida law requires insurers to provide replacement-cost coverage for residential HVAC systems damaged in a declared disaster — meaning they must pay the cost to replace the unit with a comparable modern system, not the depreciated value of the old one. This applies to homeowners' policies with replacement-cost endorsements.

### Lightning & Power Surge Claims

Florida leads the nation in lightning strikes per square mile. HVAC systems are particularly vulnerable:

**What insurance covers:**
- Compressor failure caused by a direct or nearby lightning strike (verified by weather data)
- Control board damage from a lightning-induced power surge
- Electrical failure of the condenser fan motor attributable to a surge event

**What insurance typically denies:**
- "Act of God" deductibles (usually 2% of home value in Florida — $6,000 on a $300,000 home)
- Brownout damage (gradual voltage reduction, not a sudden surge)
- Damage from repeated mini-surges over time
- Compressor failure diagnosed weeks or months after the storm event — insurers will argue the damage was pre-existing

**Pro tip:** If your AC fails after a nearby lightning strike, document the exact date and time. Your insurance company can verify lightning strike data from weather services. File the claim within days, not weeks.

### Fire & Smoke Damage

Home fires in Florida are less common than storms but more likely to result in a total system replacement:

- Heat from a fire can damage refrigerant lines (even if the lines aren't visibly burned)
- Smoke residue in ductwork often requires complete duct replacement — cleaning alone is rarely sufficient for insurance-grade remediation
- Fire-damaged electrical components may fail weeks or months after the fire (documented as "progressive failure")

Most homeowners policies cover fire damage to HVAC equipment under the dwelling coverage. Additional living expenses coverage may also apply if you need temporary cooling while repairs are underway.

### Flood Damage

Flood damage to HVAC systems is **not** covered by standard homeowners insurance. It requires a separate flood insurance policy (through NFIP or private flood insurance):

- **Ground-floor equipment** — Packaged units, ground-mounted condensers, and basement air handlers are at highest risk during flood events
- **Post-flood compressor contamination** — Floodwater entering the compressor introduces moisture, sediment, and contaminants that destroy the unit internally
- **Ductwork submersion** — Flooded ducts often require complete replacement; flood insurance policies vary on whether ductwork is covered as building property

**NFIP limit on HVAC equipment:** Under the National Flood Insurance Program, HVAC equipment and ductwork are covered up to the building coverage limits as long as they were installed at the time of the policy inception. Separate contents coverage does not apply to permanently installed equipment.

**Important:** Florida law requires insurers to accept hurricane deductibles as a separate deductible from flood deductibles. Some insurers try to apply both to a single claim — Florida Statute 627.701 prohibits double-deductible application for the same loss.

## How to File an HVAC Insurance Claim

### Step 1: Document Everything

Before contacting your insurer, gather:

- **Photos and video** of the damaged equipment, the surrounding area, and any contributing conditions (standing water, debris, visible electrical damage)
- **Weather data** — For storm or lightning claims, note the date, time, and location. Services like Weather Underground or the National Weather Service can provide official records.
- **System information** — Make, model, serial number, installation date, and maintenance records
- **Maintenance history** — Proof that the system was in good working order before the event. This is why annual [AC maintenance records](https://ac-repair.today/services/ac-maintenance/) matter — without them, insurers may classify damage as gradual wear and tear.

### Step 2: Prevent Further Damage

Insurance policies require policyholders to take reasonable steps to protect the property after a loss:

- If the system is unsafe, turn off power at the breaker
- If water is involved, have a technician pump standing water from the electrical compartment
- Arrange temporary cooling if necessary (keep receipts — this may be covered under loss-of-use provisions)
- Tarp or board up any openings caused by the damage

Failure to mitigate further damage can result in claim reduction or denial.

### Step 3: File the Claim

Contact your insurance company's claims department:

- Provide your policy number, date of loss, and a description of the damage
- Ask about the applicable deductible (hurricane deductible vs all-other-peril deductible)
- Request information about any time limits on filing or repairing
- Ask whether you need a preferred vendor or can choose your own contractor
- Get the claim number and adjuster assignment

### Step 4: The Adjuster Inspection

The insurance adjuster will inspect the equipment and determine whether the damage is covered, the extent of the loss, and the estimated payout. To prepare:

- Have the system's maintenance records available
- Request that your HVAC contractor be present during the adjuster's inspection (your contractor's report may differ from the adjuster's)
- Ask the adjuster for their measurement methodology — they may use software estimates that undercount the true scope of work
- If the adjuster says damage is pre-existing, ask for specific evidence

### Step 5: Negotiate the Estimate

Insurance company estimates are often lower than what a licensed contractor would charge for the same work:

- Get a written estimate from an [HVAC contractor in South Florida](https://ac-repair.today/services/ac-repair/)
- Compare the contractor's estimate with the adjuster's estimate line by line
- Common discrepancies: material costs, labor rates, code-compliance upgrades, and the cost of permits
- Ask the contractor to submit a supplement (an additional claim) for items the adjuster missed
- Florida law requires insurers to pay for code upgrades necessary for the repair

## Florida-Specific Insurance Protections

### Florida Statute 627.428 — Right to Attorney Fees

If your insurer denies a valid claim or underpays and you have to sue to recover, Florida law requires the insurer to pay your attorney's fees if you win. This gives Florida homeowners significant leverage in claim disputes. Most legitimate claims can be resolved through a public adjuster or attorney demand letter without a lawsuit.

### Assignment of Benefits (AOB) Reform

Florida's 2019 AOB reform (SB 122) changed how homeowners can assign their insurance benefits to contractors:

- An AOB is a document you sign transferring your right to claim payment directly to the contractor
- Under the reform, AOBs for residential property are restricted and carry additional consumer protections
- **Before signing an AOB,** verify the contractor, read the cancellation terms (you have 14 days to cancel), and understand that an AOB may trigger a more detailed claims review by your insurer
- Some legitimate HVAC contractors use AOBs; some unscrupulous ones abuse them. If you're unsure, ask to pay the contractor directly and receive reimbursement from insurance.

### Florida's "Bad Faith" Statute (624.155)

If your insurer unreasonably delays or denies payment, you may have a bad faith claim:

- The insurer must have acted without reasonable basis
- Bad faith claims are separate lawsuits from the underlying coverage dispute
- Successful bad faith claims can result in the insurer paying damages above the policy limits
- The Florida Office of Insurance Regulation tracks bad faith complaints — you can check your insurer's record before filing

## Common Florida Insurance Issues

### "AC is 15+ years old — we'll depreciate to zero"

Some insurers apply actual cash value (ACV) instead of replacement cost value (RCV) for older equipment, even on replacement-cost policies. **Push back:** Florida case law generally requires payment of replacement cost if the policy is RCV, regardless of age. Request the specific policy provision they are citing.

### "The damage was caused by lack of maintenance"

Florida insurers frequently deny HVAC claims as "wear and tear" or "lack of maintenance." The burden is on the insurer to prove that routine maintenance would have prevented the sudden failure. If you have annual maintenance records, you have a strong argument. Without them, the insurer's position is harder to defeat.

### "We only owe the contractor price, not the retail price"

Insurers sometimes try to pay based on what they claim is the "prevailing rate" or their preferred vendor's pricing. Florida law requires payment of the **reasonable cost to repair or replace**, which is the market rate charged by licensed contractors in your area — not a negotiated discount rate.
