# Common HVAC Inspection Failures in Florida

Understanding why HVAC installations fail inspection can help homeowners spot potential issues and hold contractors accountable. These are the most frequent reasons HVAC work fails final inspection in Florida.

## Top 10 Failure Reasons

### 1. Missing or Improper Secondary Drain Protection

**Code requirement**: Florida Building Code requires a secondary condensate drain or float switch to prevent overflow if the primary drain clogs.

**Common failures**:
- No float switch installed on the air handler
- Float switch installed but not wired (non-functional)
- Secondary drain terminates in the wrong location
- No visible secondary drain point to alert homeowner of primary drain failure

**Why it matters in Florida**: With near year-round cooling, condensate production is high. A clogged primary drain without backup protection can cause significant water damage to ceilings, walls, and flooring.

### 2. Unsealed Ductwork

**Code requirement**: All duct connections must be sealed with mastic or UL 181-rated tape.

**Common failures**:
- Flex duct connections using only zip ties (no mastic or tape)
- Gaps at duct board joints
- Unsealed connections at boots and plenums
- Regular duct tape used instead of UL 181 rated products

**Impact**: A typical Florida home with unsealed ducts loses 20-30% of conditioned air to the attic, significantly increasing energy costs and reducing comfort.

### 3. Incorrect Condensate Drain Slope

**Code requirement**: Minimum 1/8 inch per foot slope on horizontal condensate drain runs.

**Common failures**:
- Flat or reverse-sloped sections creating standing water
- PVC drain sagging between supports
- No cleanout access for drain line maintenance
- Drain terminates improperly (onto walkways, into storm drains)

### 4. Electrical Issues

**Code requirement**: Dedicated circuit, proper disconnect, correct wire sizing.

**Common failures**:
- Missing disconnect within sight of outdoor unit
- Disconnect sized incorrectly for the equipment amperage
- Shared circuit with other loads (not dedicated)
- Wire gauge too small for the circuit length and load
- No permit pulled for electrical work (separate trade in Florida)

### 5. Equipment Not Matching Permit

**Code requirement**: Installed equipment must match what was approved on the permit.

**Common failures**:
- Different model than specified (contractor used available stock)
- Different efficiency rating than approved
- Different capacity (tonnage) than the load calculation
- Changed equipment location without permit revision

### 6. Oversized Equipment

**Code requirement**: Equipment cannot exceed 115% of the Manual J calculated cooling load.

**Common failures**:
- 5-ton system installed where load calculation shows 3.5 tons needed
- No Manual J calculation performed at all
- Load calculation done with incorrect inputs (wrong square footage, missing insulation values)
- "Matched" to the old system without new calculation

**Why this matters in Florida**: Oversized systems cool the air quickly but don't run long enough to remove humidity. In Florida's humid climate, this means a cold, clammy house with potential mold growth, higher energy bills, and premature equipment failure from short-cycling.

### 7. Inadequate Return Air

**Code requirement**: Return air path must be adequate for the system's airflow (CFM) requirements.

**Common failures**:
- Single small return in a home that needs multiple returns
- Return air grille too small, creating high static pressure
- Blocked or restricted return air path
- No transfer grilles or jump ducts for bedrooms with closed doors

### 8. Improper Refrigerant Line Set

**Code requirement**: Properly sized, insulated, and supported refrigerant lines.

**Common failures**:
- Suction line insulation missing or inadequate
- Line set kinks or sharp bends restricting flow
- Lines not properly supported (sagging)
- Connections not properly brazed or tightened
- Line set running through unconditioned space without protection

### 9. Missing or Improper Equipment Support

**Code requirement**: Equipment must be properly supported and secured.

**Common failures**:
- Condenser not on an approved pad (sitting on bare ground or loose blocks)
- Air handler not properly suspended (inadequate hangers)
- No vibration isolation where required
- Condenser too close to property line or obstruction
- In hurricane zones: missing tie-downs or straps for condenser

### 10. Duct Insulation Deficiencies

**Code requirement**: Minimum R-6 for ducts in unconditioned spaces.

**Common failures**:
- Insulation gaps exposing bare duct to attic heat
- Insulation compressed at supports (reducing effective R-value)
- Wrong R-value installed
- Vapor barrier damaged or missing (causes condensation in humid attics)

## Pre-Inspection Checklist for Homeowners

Before your contractor calls for final inspection, walk the job yourself and check:

- [ ] New equipment secured and level
- [ ] All duct connections appear sealed (no visible gaps)
- [ ] Condensate drain appears to slope away from the unit
- [ ] Float switch visible on air handler
- [ ] Disconnect switch visible near outdoor unit
- [ ] New filter installed and accessible
- [ ] Thermostat functions (system responds to calls for cooling/heating)
- [ ] All vents have airflow
- [ ] No unusual noises during operation
- [ ] No water leaks visible after 24 hours of operation

**Note**: This is not a substitute for the official inspection, but it helps catch obvious issues early.
