# Condominium and HOA Rules for HVAC Installation in Florida

Outside the building code, Florida HVAC installations are also governed by a second layer of rules: the bylaws of condominium associations, homeowner associations (HOAs), and master-planned community boards. These private rules are enforceable in Florida, and they will sometimes restrict equipment placement, model selection, and even the timing of installation work.

This document explains how those rules interact with Florida law, what associations can and cannot prohibit, and the practical steps for getting a condo or HOA HVAC replacement approved.

## What Florida Law Says

Three Florida statutes are relevant here:

- **Florida Statute Chapter 718** — governs condominiums
- **Florida Statute Chapter 720** — governs HOAs
- **Florida Statute 553.844** — establishes the right of homeowners to certain energy-efficient improvements

Section 553.844 is the most important one for HVAC. It limits an association's ability to prohibit certain efficiency-related modifications, including some HVAC upgrades. However, the statute applies more clearly to solar collectors and rainwater systems than to traditional AC equipment, so practical enforcement varies.

## Common Restrictions Associations Enforce

In the dense South Florida condo market, the rules that show up most frequently in declarations of condominium are:

### 1. Location Restrictions on Outdoor Units

- **Balcony placement prohibitions**: Most high-rise condo boards prohibit window units and floor-standing portable AC equipment on balconies. Justifications cite aesthetics, condensate runoff onto units below, and wind-load concerns.
- **Roof access restrictions**: Some buildings prohibit unit owners from running line sets through the roof to roof-mounted condensers. In these buildings, owners are limited to the original mechanical plan.
- **Patio and yard placement (townhomes)**: HOAs often specify exactly where on a property the condenser must sit — typically along a designated side yard, screened from the street.

### 2. Equipment Model and Capacity Restrictions

- **Capacity caps**: Some condo boards limit replacement equipment to the original system's tonnage to prevent overloading common electrical infrastructure.
- **Approved manufacturers list**: A few buildings maintain pre-approved equipment lists, particularly for VRF/VRV multi-unit systems that share common piping.
- **Refrigerant type restrictions**: Older buildings sometimes restrict line set modifications, which limits owners to refrigerants compatible with existing copper.

### 3. Installation Window Restrictions

- **Quiet hours**: Most associations restrict major construction (drilling, hammering) to weekday business hours.
- **Seasonal restrictions**: Some snowbird-heavy buildings restrict noisy work during winter occupancy peak (December through April).
- **Hurricane season blackout dates**: A few buildings prohibit non-emergency exterior work during the named-storm forecast window.

### 4. Aesthetic Requirements

- **Screening requirements**: Outdoor condensers may need to be hidden behind lattice, hedge planting, or a stucco wall.
- **Paint color matching**: Some HOAs require the exterior condenser cabinet to be painted to match building trim — a request that many manufacturers' warranties prohibit.

## What Associations Generally Cannot Do

Associations operate on private contract law, but they are bound by a few limits:

- **They cannot waive permit requirements**. An association's "pre-approval" does not replace a county permit. Both are required.
- **They cannot require unlicensed work**. An association cannot force you to use a specific unlicensed handyman; Florida law requires HVAC work to be performed by a licensed contractor for jobs over the threshold. See [license-types.md](license-types.md) for details.
- **They cannot retroactively enforce new rules** against existing legal installations, in most cases. New rules typically apply only to future work.
- **They cannot prohibit certain energy-related improvements** per F.S. 553.844, though the boundaries are litigated.

## The Approval Process

For both condominiums and HOAs, the approval workflow looks roughly the same. Time required is usually 2 to 8 weeks, so plan ahead for non-emergency replacements.

### Step 1: Read the Declaration and Rules

Before scheduling any HVAC work in a covered community, locate and read:

1. The Declaration of Condominium or Declaration of Covenants
2. The Rules and Regulations (separate from the Declaration)
3. Any architectural review committee guidelines
4. Recent board meeting minutes addressing HVAC

These are public records and the association is required to provide them within 10 days of a written request.

### Step 2: Submit an Architectural Review Application

Most associations have a formal Architectural Review Application (sometimes called ARC, ARB, or Modification Request). Required documents typically include:

- Equipment specification sheets (cut sheets) for the proposed condenser and air handler
- Site plan showing the equipment location
- Photos of the existing installation
- Contractor's license and insurance certificates
- Florida Product Approval (FL#) for outdoor equipment
- Manufacturer's warranty documentation

### Step 3: Board Review

The review window is set in the Declaration but commonly 30 days. Associations are required to provide a written response. If they fail to respond within the deadline, the application is sometimes deemed approved by default (check your specific Declaration language).

### Step 4: County Permit

Association approval is a separate step from county permitting. Once you have association sign-off, the licensed contractor pulls the county permit per the normal [permit process](permit-process.md).

### Step 5: Installation and Inspection

The contractor installs, the county inspects, and the homeowner submits the final inspection report back to the association to close out the approval.

## Special Case: Commercial and Mixed-Use Buildings

Mixed-use buildings (commercial ground floor, residential upper floors) and condotel properties have additional rules. The commercial portion is usually governed by a separate condominium association, and any tenant-level HVAC modifications need that association's approval. For commercial properties, the equipment threshold for licensing is also different — a contractor who is licensed for residential may not be licensed for the larger units commonly used in commercial spaces.

Property managers and condo boards needing licensed [commercial HVAC service](https://ac-repair.today/services/commercial-hvac/) for shared mechanical systems should verify that the contractor's license class covers the equipment tonnage being serviced.

## What to Do If Your Association Is Unreasonable

A handful of practical paths:

1. **Owner meeting and vote**: Many objectionable rules can be amended by a majority owner vote. If the rule isn't in the Declaration itself (only in board-adopted rules), the threshold is lower.
2. **DBPR Division of Florida Condominiums, Timeshares, and Mobile Homes**: Files complaints against condo associations for violations of Chapter 718.
3. **Mediation through DBPR**: F.S. 720.311 requires mandatory pre-suit mediation for many HOA disputes.
4. **Civil litigation**: For unreasonable restraints on alienation or violations of statute, owners have a right to sue. This is expensive and slow but sometimes the only path.

## Practical Tips From Experienced Contractors

- **Submit early in the season**. Boards process more requests in spring and summer; turnaround is slower.
- **Include extra documentation**. A board faced with a thick, organized packet is more likely to approve than one looking at a one-page form.
- **Be specific about hours**. If your installation will fit within standard hours, say so explicitly. Vague applications get pushed back for clarification.
- **Build in a slip month**. If your AC has died and you're already in a heat emergency, ask the board chair for an expedited review citing health concerns.
