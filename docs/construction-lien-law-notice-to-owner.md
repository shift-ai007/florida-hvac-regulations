# Florida Construction Lien Law & the Notice to Owner: An HVAC Homeowner's Guide

When you hire a contractor to replace your AC system or install new ductwork, you reasonably assume that paying the contractor in full ends your financial obligation. In Florida, that assumption can be wrong. Under **Florida's Construction Lien Law (Chapter 713, Florida Statutes)**, you can be forced to pay *twice* for the same work if your contractor doesn't pay their suppliers or subcontractors. This guide explains how that happens on HVAC jobs and how to protect yourself.

> **Disclaimer:** This is general information, not legal advice. Construction lien law is technical and the statutes change. For a specific dispute, consult a Florida construction attorney.

## Why HVAC Jobs Are Exposed to Liens

A typical AC replacement involves more parties than the homeowner sees:

- The **contractor** you signed with and paid
- An **equipment distributor** who supplied the condenser, air handler, and coil
- Possibly a **subcontractor** for electrical, crane, or duct fabrication
- Suppliers of refrigerant line sets, materials, and parts

Each of these parties — anyone who furnishes labor, services, or materials that improve your property — has a potential right to file a **construction lien** (sometimes called a mechanic's lien) against your home if they aren't paid. Critically, *their right exists even if you paid your contractor in full.* If the contractor takes your money and fails to pay the equipment distributor, that distributor can lien your house.

## The Notice to Owner (NTO)

The mechanism that makes this real is the **Notice to Owner**. Under §713.06, a supplier or subcontractor who does not have a direct contract with you must serve you a Notice to Owner — generally within **45 days** of first furnishing materials or labor — to preserve their lien rights.

An NTO is **not** a bill and **not** a sign your contractor did anything wrong. It's a routine, legally required heads-up that says, in effect: "We are supplying materials/labor for your project; make sure we get paid." Receiving one on a larger HVAC install is normal. **Do not ignore it.** It tells you exactly who must be paid before you make final payment.

## How Homeowners Get Hit — and How to Prevent It

The double-payment trap closes like this: you pay the contractor 100%, the contractor pockets it, the equipment distributor never gets paid, and the distributor — who properly served an NTO — records a lien on your home. To clear title (for example, to sell or refinance) you may have to pay the distributor directly.

Your primary defense is the **Release of Lien / Waiver**. Before releasing payment — especially the final payment — collect lien releases from every party who could lien the property:

- **Contractor's final affidavit (§713.06(3)(d)):** On a contract with a direct contractor, you are entitled to demand a **Contractor's Final Payment Affidavit** at least 5 days before final payment. It lists everyone still owed money. Withholding final payment until you receive it is one of your strongest protections.
- **Partial releases** for progress payments and **a final release** at completion, signed by the contractor and by anyone who served an NTO.
- **Conditional vs. unconditional releases:** A *conditional* release is valid only once the check clears; an *unconditional* release is final. Match the release type to the payment status.

Practical rule of thumb for HVAC jobs: **don't make final payment until you hold lien releases from the contractor and every NTO sender, plus the contractor's final payment affidavit.**

## The Notice of Commencement

For permitted HVAC work above the statutory threshold, Florida requires a **Notice of Commencement (§713.13)** to be recorded and posted at the job site before work begins. It identifies the owner, contractor, and property. Posting it matters because it protects *you*: improper or absent recording can expose the owner to paying lienors even after paying the contractor. For most straightforward AC change-outs your contractor handles this as part of the permit process, but on larger projects confirm it was recorded.

## Timeline at a Glance

| Event | Deadline | Who acts |
|-------|----------|----------|
| Notice of Commencement recorded | Before work begins | Owner/contractor |
| Notice to Owner served | Within 45 days of first furnishing | Supplier/subcontractor |
| Contractor's final payment affidavit | ≥5 days before final payment | Contractor (on owner demand) |
| Claim of Lien recorded | Within 90 days of last furnishing | Unpaid party |
| Lawsuit to enforce lien | Within 1 year of recording the lien | Lienor |

## How This Connects to Hiring a Licensed Contractor

Lien exposure is one more reason to vet your contractor carefully. A reputable, properly licensed and insured HVAC company pays its distributors and subs on time, provides lien releases without being chased, and records the Notice of Commencement correctly. Unlicensed or fly-by-night operators are exactly the ones who collect payment and stiff suppliers — leaving the lien on your house. Before signing, verify the license through the [Florida DBPR](https://www.myfloridalicense.com) and review the related [questions to ask before hiring](docs/hiring-checklist.md) and your broader [homeowner rights and protections](docs/homeowner-rights.md).

## Key Takeaways for HVAC Homeowners

1. Paying your contractor in full does **not** automatically protect you — unpaid suppliers can still lien your home.
2. A Notice to Owner is routine; read it and note who must be paid.
3. Demand the Contractor's Final Payment Affidavit before final payment.
4. Collect signed lien releases from the contractor and every NTO sender before releasing the last check.
5. Confirm the Notice of Commencement was recorded on permitted work.
6. Hiring a licensed, insured, reputable contractor is the single best way to avoid the problem entirely.
