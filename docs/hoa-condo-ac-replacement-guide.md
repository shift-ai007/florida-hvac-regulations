# HOA and Condo Association AC Replacement Rules in Florida

If you live in a single-family home with no homeowners association, replacing your air conditioner is a straightforward decision: pick the system, hire a licensed contractor, and schedule the installation. But if your home falls under an HOA or a condominium association — as hundreds of thousands of South Florida homes do — the process is significantly more complicated. Associations have authority over exterior equipment placement, noise levels, installation timing, equipment screening, and even which brands or colors are acceptable. This guide covers what you need to know before you replace your AC in a governed community.

## Does Your HOA Have Authority Over Your AC?

The short answer is yes — but the scope varies. Florida law (Chapter 718 for condos, Chapter 720 for HOAs) gives associations authority over the common elements and the exterior appearance of units. Since an AC system involves outdoor condensers, line sets running along exterior walls, and sometimes rooftop equipment, virtually every AC replacement touches something the association controls.

**What most associations govern:**
- Outdoor unit location and placement
- Appearance of line-set covers on exterior walls
- Noise levels and operating hours (some have nuisance clauses)
- Condenser pad height and materials (especially in flood zones)
- Contractor licensing and insurance minimums
- Installation timing and advance notice

**What associations generally do not govern:**
- The indoor equipment or brand choice (unless it affects appearance)
- Ductwork inside your unit
- Thermostat selection
- Efficiency level or SEER rating

The line between what is governed and what is not varies by association. Some HOAs in Miami-Dade require ACC (Architectural Control Committee) approval for any exterior change including AC replacement; others only regulate fence and paint colors. The only way to know is to read your governing documents — or call your property manager.

## The Approval Process — Step by Step

### Step 1: Read Your Documents

Your HOA's Declaration of Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions (CC&Rs) or your condo's Declaration of Condominium will specify which exterior changes require prior approval. Look for sections titled "Architectural Control," "Exterior Alterations," or "Maintenance and Repair." If you cannot find explicit language about HVAC systems, assume that any change visible from the exterior requires approval.

### Step 2: Prepare the Application

Most associations use a standard architectural alteration request form. For an AC replacement, you typically need to supply:

- **Contractor information** — Name, license number (CAC), certificate of insurance ($1M general liability + workers' comp minimum)
- **Equipment specifications** — Make, model, tonnage, SEER2 rating, compressor type
- **Site plan** — A simple diagram showing where the outdoor unit will sit, the line-set path, and the distance from property lines and windows
- **Color and screening** — The condenser cabinet color and any planned screening (shrubbery, fence, or lattice)
- **Permit documentation** — Most associations want to see the building permit application before they issue association approval

### Step 3: Submit and Wait

Florida law requires associations to respond within 30 days (though many respond faster). If you do not receive a response within 30 days, the request is automatically approved in many associations — but do not rely on this without confirming your association's bylaws.

### Step 4: Schedule the Installation

Once you have both association approval and a county building permit, you can schedule the work. You must coordinate with the association on timing if the installation requires:
- Access to a common-element rooftop (condos)
- Shutting down power to a common area
- Temporary access through locked gates or buildings
- Elevator use for equipment transport (high-rise condos)

## Special Condominium Considerations

Condos have additional layers of complexity that single-family HOA homes do not:

**Rooftop units.** If your AC condenser sits on the roof (common in mid-rise and high-rise buildings), the association owns the roof. You cannot authorize a contractor to walk on it without association permission. The association may also have a list of approved roofing contractors who are insured to work on the roof membrane, and your HVAC contractor may need to subcontract or coordinate with them.

**Through-wall units.** Many older Miami Beach and Fort Lauderdale condos use through-wall packaged terminal air conditioners (PTACs). Replacing a PTAC requires matching the sleeve dimensions exactly — a mistake means cutting into the building structure, which is a structural alteration and requires both association and city approval.

**Water-cooled systems.** Some high-rise condos use central chilled water or water-source heat pumps rather than individual air-cooled condensers. If your building uses a central cooling plant, you cannot "replace the outdoor unit" because there isn't one — any maintenance or upgrade goes through the association's facilities team and is billed as a common-element repair.

**Flood zone requirements.** If your outdoor unit sits at ground level in a flood zone (AE or VE on FEMA maps), the condenser pad must be elevated above the base flood elevation. Some associations have stricter elevation requirements than the building code. An installation that puts a new condenser at ground level where the old one was may still be out of compliance if flood maps have been updated since the old unit was installed.

## Noise Ordinances and Nuisance Rules

South Florida's density means your outdoor condenser is likely close to a neighbor's window, patio, or bedroom wall. Most municipalities have noise ordinances (typically 55-60 dBA daytime, 50-55 dBA nighttime at the property line), and many HOAs have their own nuisance clauses that are stricter.

Modern inverter-driven compressors from premium lines (Carrier Greenspeed, Trane XV, Lennox SL Series) are significantly quieter than older single-stage units — often 55 dBA vs 72 dBA from 10 feet. If your outdoor unit sits near a property line, spending more on a quiet condenser can prevent a neighbor complaint and an HOA nuisance violation.

## Documentation to Keep

After you complete the replacement, maintain a file with:

- The association architectural approval letter
- The building permit and final inspection sign-off
- The contractor's license and insurance certificate
- The manufacturer warranty registration
- The equipment model and serial numbers
- Photographs of the installation

This documentation matters when you sell the home. A buyer's title company or attorney will ask whether exterior improvements had proper approvals, and missing association approval can delay or derail a closing. ## Common Pitfalls

- **Assuming the new unit can go in the same spot.** Flood maps change, setback requirements change, and the old unit may have been a nonconforming installation that gets flagged when you apply for a permit.
- **Skipping the HOA approval and submitting after the fact.** The association cannot reject an installed unit that meets code, but it can fine you for violating the approval process, and it can require you to screen or move the unit at your expense.
- **Using a contractor who is not on the association's approved list.** Some condos maintain a list of pre-vetted contractors who have demonstrated proper insurance and roofing access protocols. Using a contractor outside this list may be denied outright.
- **Ordering the unit before getting approval.** If approval takes three weeks and the unit arrives in one week, you are storing a large appliance and paying an early-payment fee to the distributor for a system you cannot install yet.

Replacing an AC in a governed community takes longer and costs a bit more in administrative overhead than an ungoverned installation. But a smooth process — with approvals and permits lined up before the crew arrives — means you go from sweating to comfortable in one visit rather than the two or three it takes when the installation gets halfway done and a manager stops the work.
